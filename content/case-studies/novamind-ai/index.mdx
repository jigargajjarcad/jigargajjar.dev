## Context and constraints

NovaMind was a deliberate learning project, and the case study is written on that
basis rather than around a product claim it cannot support.

The goal was to build a retrieval-augmented generation system end to end and
understand every layer of it — document ingestion, chunking, embeddings, vector
search, reranking, streaming generation, citation mapping, authentication, cost
tracking, and deployment — instead of assembling managed services or following a
tutorial. The question being answered was a personal one:

> Can I design and build an end-to-end document platform that follows production
> engineering practices, rather than one that remains a proof of concept?

The intended user is someone with their own collection of documents who wants to
question it in natural language **and check the answer**. Grounding responses in
uploaded material, with a citation pointing at the passage that supports each
claim, was the product requirement that shaped the retrieval design.

**The constraints were time and scope.** The repository was created on 15 June
2026 and last pushed on 24 June 2026 — nine days of commits, by a single
engineer, alongside other work. Scope was set at one complete vertical slice
rather than breadth: upload, ingest, retrieve, answer, cite, and the accounts and
cost tracking a real deployment needs.

**What was deliberately left out** was abstraction. Managed retrieval services
and higher-level RAG frameworks were avoided on purpose, because using them would
have skipped the part the project existed to learn.

<Callout variant="critical" heading="What was not deliberately left out">
  Automated tests. They are absent from this project, and their absence was not a considered
  trade-off — evaluation simply never became part of the work while attention went to architecture,
  retrieval design, and deployment. The Verification section below describes what was done instead,
  and what that does and does not catch. It is the weakest part of the project and it is not
  presented as anything else.
</Callout>

## Architecture

The backend separates concerns along a conventional boundary, and the separation
is real rather than nominal — each layer is a directory with one job.

```
api/       routes, schemas          the HTTP boundary
core/      config, exceptions, security
db/        database, repositories   persistence
models/    user, collection, document, chat
services/  ingestion · rag · anthropic · chat · collection · document · auth · email
```

A repository layer sits between services and the database, so a service composes
behaviour and never writes a query. `rag_service` and `anthropic_service` are
separate on purpose: retrieval and generation fail in different ways and are
worth being able to inspect independently.

**Ingestion.** PDFs are parsed with PyMuPDF and split paragraph-aware into
512-token chunks with 50 tokens of overlap, so a chunk boundary is less likely to
fall through the middle of an argument.

**Retrieval.** The question is embedded with Voyage `voyage-3` at 1024 dimensions
and matched against stored chunk vectors by HNSW index in pgvector, returning ten
candidates. Those ten are reranked by `rerank-2-lite`, and the best five become
the model's context.

**Generation and citation.** The five passages are passed to Claude
`sonnet-4-6` as numbered context blocks, and the response streams back. Numbered
references in the output are resolved to their source chunk, and both the message
and its citations are persisted — so a citation remains checkable after the fact
rather than existing only in the rendered reply.

**Persistence.** PostgreSQL 15 with pgvector, accessed through SQLAlchemy's async
layer with Alembic migrations. Users, collections, documents, chunks, embeddings,
chat history and cost records are one database.

**Authentication.** JWT with HS256 and a seven-day expiry, with email
verification and password reset delivered through Resend.

**Deployment.** Docker Compose for local development; the client is deployed to
Vercel and is reachable.

## Key decisions

<Decision
  context="A retrieval system needs somewhere to put vectors. The reflexive answer is a purpose-built vector database, and most published examples reach for one."
  choice="Store embeddings in the same PostgreSQL instance as the application data, using pgvector with an HNSW index."
  alternatives="Pinecone, Qdrant, or Weaviate as a separate service alongside Postgres."
  consequence="One database to deploy, monitor and back up, and transactional consistency between a document, its metadata and its embeddings — they commit together or not at all. The cost is accepted rather than hidden: a dedicated index would very likely serve a far larger corpus better, and this is not the choice I would defend for internet-scale search. For a system whose goal was a clean end-to-end architecture, fewer moving parts was worth more than headroom that would never be used."
/>

<Decision
  context="Vector similarity reliably returns relevant chunks. It does not reliably return the best chunks, and the model sees only what it is given."
  choice="Retrieve ten candidates by vector search, rerank them with Voyage `rerank-2-lite`, and pass the top five to the model. Most implementations pass the vector results straight through."
  alternatives="Send the top-k similarity results directly to the model, saving a network round trip and its latency on every query."
  consequence="Answers became noticeably more focused and cited passages more relevant, judged by repeated manual comparison on a fixed document set rather than by benchmark. The cost is a second model call in the critical path of every question. For a system whose product requirement is a verifiable answer rather than the fastest possible one, that trade was worth making — but the evidence for it is observational, and the section below says so."
/>

Two further choices are worth stating precisely, because they were adopted rather
than tested.

**Voyage embeddings** were chosen because they are built for retrieval and pair
with the same vendor's reranker. No comparison against OpenAI or open-weight
embeddings was run. It was an informed selection, not a measured one.

**512-token chunks with 50 tokens of overlap** began as a common default. Chunk
boundaries were inspected during manual verification and the retrieved context
stayed coherent, so the values were kept — but no systematic experiment across
chunk sizes or overlaps was carried out. They are defaults that were watched, not
parameters that were tuned.

## Failures and mistakes

### The local environment was hiding four assumptions at once

Under Docker Compose everything worked. The frontend, backend, database and
environment were all under my control, and I assumed without examining it that a
real deployment would behave the same way.

It did not. The first deployment surfaced four failures more or less
simultaneously:

- The frontend still fell back to localhost defaults where a production URL
  should have been required.
- CORS behaved differently once the client and the API were on separate origins.
- Client-side routes worked locally because the dev server resolved them
  automatically; direct navigation failed in production without a rewrite rule.
- Environment variables that had been optional locally were mandatory in
  production, and their absence failed quietly rather than loudly.

None was difficult alone. Arriving together, they took roughly a day to work
through — reproducing each failure against the deployed system, separating
application faults from infrastructure ones, and removing assumptions one at a
time until behaviour was consistent.

The corrections stayed as architecture rather than patches. Production URLs are
now required instead of silently defaulting; CORS names its deployment origins
explicitly; client-side routing is backed by an explicit rewrite rule; and
environment configuration is treated as part of the system's design rather than
as setup.

The lesson is not about CORS or any particular host. **A local environment hides
assumptions, and a deployment is frequently the first place they become visible.**
I now think about the target environment much earlier, and try to remove
environment-specific assumptions before they can become production bugs.

## Verification

**There is no automated test suite.** `backend/tests/` contains an empty
`__init__.py` and a `conftest.py`, and nothing else. The repository carries a CI
workflow with four jobs, and two of them run test commands against suites that do
not exist. That is the honest state of it.

What existed instead was a manual loop, run deliberately rather than casually.

A fixed set of representative documents and a fixed set of questions were kept,
and re-run by hand after every change to chunking, retrieval limits, or ranking.
For each response, three things were checked:

1. Whether the retrieved passages actually contained the information needed to
   answer the question.
2. Whether the reranker promoted the genuinely most relevant chunks into the
   final context.
3. Whether every citation pointed at a passage that supported the claim it was
   attached to.

The habit that made this useful was **inspecting the retrieved chunks rather than
reading the answer** — a fluent response tells you very little about whether
retrieval worked, and reading the context tells you why an answer was right or
wrong. This did catch retrieval quality shifting after changes to chunking and
ranking parameters, which is the failure the loop existed to find.

<Callout variant="critical" heading="What a manual loop cannot do">
  It cannot run on every change, it does not scale past a small document set, and it cannot detect a
  regression nobody thought to look for. Its coverage is exactly the questions someone remembered to
  ask. No case was observed where an answer looked correct while its citation referenced unrelated
  content — but with no automated regression suite, that is an absence of observation rather than
  evidence of absence, and it is not a claim that such failures could not occur.
</Callout>

## Outcomes

There are no usage outcomes. **The system has never been used by anyone other
than its author** — no external users, no traffic, no production incidents. Any
figure describing retrieval accuracy, latency, or adoption would be invented, so
none appears here.

What can be stated is what the project reached, and where it stopped.

<Metric
  value="9"
  unit="days"
  label="First commit to deployed"
  qualifier="15 to 24 June 2026, alongside other work"
/>

**It was deployed and it is reachable.** The client is live on Vercel and the
full path — upload, ingest, retrieve, rerank, answer, cite — runs end to end.

**The architecture goal was met.** Ingestion, retrieval, generation and
persistence are separated behind a repository boundary, and that is checkable by
reading the source rather than by taking this document's word for it.

**The verification goal was not met.** Validation stayed manual for the life of
the project, and the absence of an automated suite is the single largest gap
between what this system is and what "production-ready" would require.

The lifecycle badge reads `prototype` for that reason. It is deployed, it works,
and it is not maintained — the last commit is 24 June 2026.

## Looking back

**The first thing I would add is the evaluation.** A fixed set of questions with
known-good passages, scored automatically on every change, would have replaced the
manual loop with something that runs whether or not anyone remembers to run it.
Everything else in the project would have been easier to change afterwards,
because changing retrieval parameters would have stopped being a decision made
partly on impression.

**The thing I would redesign is environment configuration.** Treating it as setup
rather than as part of the architecture is what allowed four assumptions to
survive undetected until the first deployment. Requiring explicit configuration
from the start, and failing loudly on its absence, costs almost nothing at the
beginning and saved nothing by being deferred.

**Starting today, I would deploy earlier and with less in it.** The failures in
this project were not in the retrieval design, which held up; they were in the
distance between the machine it was built on and the environment it had to run
in. A deployment on day two would have surfaced all four of them while the system
was small enough that they were trivial.

The claims in this document are traceable to the repository or stated as
observation. Where verification was manual, it says so, and where a choice was
adopted rather than measured, it says that too.
